LISA / UKRAINE WAR UPDATE

What Is Actually Happening in Ukraine? The October 2026 Update

An assessment of Ukraine's renewed counteroffensives, deteriorating relations with Trump and the USA, the struggle over Russian energy infrastructure, North Korea's expanding military role, and renewed diplomatic efforts amid continued Russian attacks.

Austin Burgess · Chief Analyst – Ukraine, LISA · Nov. 14, 2026 · 9 min read

September and October in Ukraine have been among the bloodiest periods of the war. Not only did Russia suffer a staggering 54,400 personnel losses and 50,222 UAV losses in October according to the Ukrainian General Staff. Using available estimates, Russia suffered approximately 1,077 reported personnel losses for every net square kilometre of Ukrainian territory gained during the corresponding reporting periods. LISA’s October assessment explains the slowing of Russian advances, the back-and-forth relations between Washington, Moscow, and Kyiv, and what we can anticipate going into the winter offensives.

01

Ukraine's Counteroffensives Challenge Russia's Claims of Battlefield Progress

Independent analysis from various sources exposed a significant disagreement between Russian claims of territorial advances. For most of the summer, many questioned how fast Russia could grind through the Donetsk Oblast. By late October, that question changed to whether Russia was advancing at all.

The stall is not only a Russian failure; it is the result of Operation Vivaldi, as well as other counteroffensives executed by Ukrainian forces, with information silence being a major driver in making these counteroffensives a success. Many analysts, including some Russian mil-bloggers, have admitted that Ukraine has regained the initiative in several sectors.

On November 1, Zelenskyy announced that Ukrainian forces had liberated 96.2 km² during October, approximately 10.4 km² more than Russian forces captured. However, a separate territorial tracker using successive DeepState map snapshots recorded 148.5 km² of Russian advances and 83.2 km² of Ukrainian advances, suggesting a net Russian gain of approximately 65.3 km². Preston Stewart, an American military analyst, says the net Russian gains could be as low as or lower than 7 square kilometres.

To put Russia's reported losses into perspective, consider an NFL-sized football field, including both end zones. For every equivalent field-sized area of net Ukrainian territory Russia gained, it incurred approximately 4.4 reported personnel losses.

The Kremlin’s Distorted Picture of the Battlefield

There is growing evidence that Russia’s military leadership is either feeding the Kremlin an increasingly distorted picture of battlefield progress, or knowingly presenting figures that don’t reflect reality. On October 16, Chief of the General Staff Valery Gerasimov told Vladimir Putin that Russian forces had captured more than 1,800 km² and 92 settlements since September 1. However, the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) assessed that Russian forces had suffered a net territorial loss of 122 km² over the same period.

Many sources have repeatedly identified exaggerated battlefield reporting as part of a broader effort to portray Russian advances as inevitable.

The discrepancy between reported territorial control and reality risks creating a serious command-and-control problem for Russia. If senior leadership is making operational decisions based on overstated gains, Russia risks misallocating reinforcements, committing troops to positions that remain contested, and diverting resources to recapture territory it was previously told had been secured.

Even though Russia is experiencing problems translating claims into actual battlefield control, Ukraine is also facing uncertainty over whether its most important military partner will continue providing critical capabilities.

02

The USA’s Support Is Becoming a Strategic Vulnerability for Kyiv

October showed how much Ukraine’s military operations depend on decisions made in Washington. Relations between Trump and Zelenskyy deteriorated over disagreements surrounding Ukrainian attacks on Russian energy infrastructure, as well as the direction of peace negotiations. What began as diplomatic friction quickly translated into direct pressure on Ukraine’s military.

One of the clearest examples of this dependence emerged through restrictions affecting SpaceX's Starlink and Starshield satellite communications systems. In October, Ukrainian forces experienced disruptions affecting military-grade satellite connectivity, particularly systems used in drone operations. The restrictions reportedly limited the ability of certain high-speed drones to maintain connections, creating complications for Ukrainian strike missions and air defence operations.

The growing division became very apparent on October 9, when Trump announced an agreement with Putin for Russia to supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to Americans and international markets, shortly followed by announcements of 500,000 tonnes in November and one million tonnes thereafter. Simultaneously, sanctions on Russian fuel imports were temporarily eased. Zelenskyy criticized the arrangement, arguing that providing additional revenue to Moscow would not encourage Russia to end its invasion.

Washington and Kyiv are increasingly demonstrating that they do not necessarily share the same objectives for ending the war. While Ukraine seeks to weaken Russia's ability to sustain its invasion, Washington's pursuit of economic agreements and renewed negotiations with Moscow can create competing priorities that constrain Ukrainian strategy.

03

Ukraine's Weak Points Are Interceptors, Warning Time and Connectivity

Ukraine's long-range drones are no longer just hitting oil. In October they reached the systems that let Russia's economy and military run. Ukraine's most serious problems in October were things it cannot build itself: Interceptors, Warning Time, and Connectivity. Many of these things may be connected to the growing tension between the USA and Ukraine.

Interceptors

Zelensky said the United States delivers only 12 Patriot interceptors a month, and that Ukraine received 24 over 90 days against a need of about 350 a month. Russia fired 100 or more missiles against Kyiv (approximately 300 km from the nearest front line in the Sumy direction).

Ukraine had waited nearly a month for US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to sign off on a batch of Patriot and NASAMS missiles that allies had already paid for. Marco Rubio acknowledged this delay and claimed it was not political. Zelenskyy also said several European countries are ready to transfer their own interceptors, but that was pending US approval.

Warning Time

ISW observed that Ukraine received no early warning before Russian missile strikes on the nights of October 12 to 13, 13 to 14, and 18 to 19. The United States denied cutting off intelligence sharing; by October 20 Zelensky said the warnings were working again.

Connectivity

On October 14, a speed limit of 160 km/h appeared on SpaceX's Starshield terminals on moving platforms. ISW reports this has disrupted Ukraine's jet-powered interceptor drones and degraded its mid-range strike campaign against Russian logistics. Who ordered it is disputed: Rubio says Starlink decisions rest with SpaceX and that Ukraine never had Starshield, while Elon Musk wrote that limits on Starshield are set by the US military and ultimately the president. A bipartisan group of US senators has asked the Pentagon's inspector general to investigate. Whether the cap has been lifted remains contested.

These vulnerabilities compound one another. Interceptor shortages weaken air defence, intelligence disruptions reduce warning time, and connectivity restrictions undermine drone operations. Together, they risk limiting Ukraine's ability to defend its cities while sustaining offensive pressure against Russia.

04

Russia Is Paying More for Fewer Soldiers

Ukraine's General Staff says Russia's cumulative losses rose from 1,488,200 at the end of August to 1,552,840 on October 11, roughly 1,580 per day over those 41 days. ISW separately says Russia has lost more than 40,000 killed and wounded over several months of this summer and autumn.

The cost of replacing the soldiers is rising. ISW says contract recruitment has become so expensive that Putin is reportedly reluctantly considering another unpopular involuntary reservist call-up, something the President of Russia has avoided since 2022. Although there have not been reports of mobilization, Russia's routine autumn conscription is planned at about 120,000 men, down from 135,000 in autumn 2025, with a promise they will not be sent to fight in Ukraine.

Furthermore, Ukraine says Operation Vivaldi so badly damaged Russia's 144th Motorized Rifle Division that it effectively ceased to exist as an organized force.

October 2026 marked a new record for Russian personnel losses, with approximately 54,400 reported killed, wounded or otherwise removed from combat. That represents approximately 73 personnel losses every hour. October also surpassed the previous monthly record, set in December 2024, by nearly 12%. More significantly, ISW assessed that Russian forces actually lost a net 27.29 km² of territory during the month. Russia was therefore suffering its highest reported monthly losses of the war while simultaneously losing ground.

05

Russia's Shadow War on Europe Is Growing Bolder

While Russia struggles in Donetsk, it is escalating below the threshold of open war against NATO members. October saw growing concern among European governments over suspected Russian sabotage, airspace violations, attacks on commercial shipping and efforts to disrupt infrastructure supporting Ukraine. The objective is to create instability, impose additional security costs and weaken the political willingness of European governments to continue supporting Kyiv.

The Black Sea became a particularly concerning example. In two separate operations during October, Ukraine accused Russian forces of using aerial and maritime drones to sink four commercial cargo vessels outside Bulgaria's territorial waters. Bulgaria is both an EU and NATO member. According to Eurovision News Spotlight, two crew members had been confirmed dead and at least ten others were missing and presumed dead since the beginning of October. The incidents raised additional concerns about the security of commercial shipping routes used for Ukrainian grain exports.

On October 5, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas publicly warned of an increase in Russian hybrid attacks, sabotage attempts and airspace violations. She also highlighted Russian missiles and drones repeatedly entering Moldovan airspace, including three drones reported by Moldova's Defence Ministry on September 30.

Russia's suspected hybrid operations are creating a second layer of pressure around the war, targeting the countries, infrastructure and supply networks that enable Ukraine to continue fighting. By disrupting shipping, testing airspace security and targeting defence-related logistics, Moscow can impose costs on European governments without necessarily crossing the threshold of a conventional military confrontation with NATO. The strategic concern for Ukraine is that even without a direct attack on a NATO member, sustained instability across Europe could divert resources, weaken political confidence and make continued military support for Kyiv increasingly difficult.

06

Russia Is Preparing Ukraine for a Winter of Energy Warfare

Russia is not simply trying to knock out Ukraine’s power stations. It is attacking the network that makes electricity, heating and water available to entire cities. That is a more serious problem than the number of damaged power plants alone suggests. As both military powers prepare for their fourth winter, Russian forces have transitioned their targets to increasingly include the interconnected systems responsible for generating and distributing electricity, supplying heating and maintaining water services. With winter approaching, the damage threatens to place additional pressure on Ukrainian civilians, industry and the country's ability to sustain essential services.

An October 14 attack on Kyiv demonstrated the consequences of targeting facilities that serve more than one purpose. Russian ballistic missiles struck all three of the city's combined heat and power plants, which provide both electricity and heating. Ukrainian officials also reported damage to other energy facilities, leaving approximately 600,000 people without electricity. A further wave of attacks on October 18–19 damaged 28 substations and other energy infrastructure across 15 oblasts, causing outages affecting approximately 550,000 households.

The consequences of the civilian infrastructure strikes also extend beyond lighting and household appliances. More than 30% of Ukrainian households rely on district heating, and combined heat and power plants are essential to supplying it in major cities. Electricity is also required to operate water pumps, heating controls and communications infrastructure. When power is interrupted, these services can be affected together. Households may turn to electric heaters when district heating fails, placing additional demand on an already strained electricity system. In severe cases, prolonged interruptions can damage water and heating infrastructure in ways that are not immediately resolved when electricity returns.

Russia's energy campaign is designed to impose pressure beyond the immediate damage caused by individual strikes. Repeated attacks on generation, transmission and heating infrastructure increase the risk that electricity disruptions will spread into water services, communications, industrial production and everyday civilian life. The approaching winter makes those vulnerabilities more consequential. Ukraine has improved its ability to distribute generation, protect facilities and restore damaged infrastructure, but these measures cannot eliminate the risks created by a sustained campaign against a system with limited operational capacity. Russia may not need to disable Ukraine's entire electricity network to achieve strategic effects. Prolonged outages, higher repair costs, reduced industrial activity and the burden placed on civilians can all become instruments of pressure on the Ukrainian government and its international supporters.
